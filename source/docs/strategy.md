# Part I — The recommendation

## The answer in three sentences

WeYield today sells rate optimisation to revenue managers, who are operators with a tool budget. It should sell fleet P&L optimisation to owners, who have a profit-and-loss account. AI is not the product — AI is the only thing that makes it economically possible to serve 600 to 1,000 fragmented operators with a product that has previously required a consulting engagement per client.

## Three changes, in strict sequence

| | Change | From | To |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **The object** | The rate | The fleet plan — five levers, not one |
| **2** | **The buyer** | Revenue manager (tool budget, €12.5K) | Owner / general manager (P&L, €50K+) |
| **3** | **The cost to serve** | Consultant-led implementation and coaching | AI-native onboarding, configuration and advisory |

Change 3 is what makes 1 and 2 affordable. Change 1 is what makes 2 credible. They are not independent workstreams and they cannot be run in parallel.

# Part II — Why the present product is structurally capped

## 2.1 Car rental is not hotel revenue management, and WeYield has been built as though it is

The two businesses look identical from the outside — rate shopping, forecasting, an RMS, a human revenue manager in the middle — and they are structurally different in the one way that matters.

**In hotels, inventory is fixed.** Room 301 exists whether or not it is sold, it cannot be moved to another city, and it cannot be sold off at the end of the season. Rate is the only meaningful lever. An RMS that optimises rate is therefore a *complete* product.

**In car rental, inventory is mobile, substitutable and disposable.** The operator has five levers:

| Lever | The decision | Who makes it today |
|---|---|---|
| **Price** | Rate by car group, channel, booking window, length of rental | Revenue manager — *WeYield's user* |
| **Move** | Transfer units between stations to meet peak demand | Operations, by instinct |
| **Substitute** | Walk the customer up the ACRISS ladder (ECMR → CDMR) | Counter staff, ad hoc |
| **Buy** | How many of each group, when, at what acquisition cost | Owner, annually, by gut |
| **Sell** | When to de-fleet each cohort | Owner, by gut or by lease expiry |

**WeYield optimises the first lever and — by its own site's account — plans the fleet against demand for the revenue manager** ("fleet and price optimization"; Performance Hub and Revenue Horizon both analyse fleet). What no product in this market does is optimise the fleet for *value*: what each cohort is worth today, and when to sell it. Pricing plus demand-side fleet planning is a fraction of a five-lever problem, and that gap is the mechanical reason WeYield's ACV sits at €12,500 and why the addressable ceiling is roughly €7.5M — not a sales-coverage problem and not an AI problem.

## 2.2 For franchisees, WeYield is selling partial control of the weakest lever

A meaningful share of the installed base are franchisees of Hertz, Avis, Europcar, Budget or Sixt. Franchisees pay a royalty on revenue, receive the brand and the reservation feed, and **do not hold full rate autonomy** — the franchisor sets or constrains significant parts of the rate structure.

So for a large part of the base, WeYield is selling optimisation of a lever the customer only partly controls, while the four levers they control completely go untouched. This is worth verifying account by account, because it directly predicts which customers will pay for the fleet product first.

## 2.3 The TAM arithmetic

**Given:** ~120 customers, 600–1,000 addressable, €1.5M revenue.
**Derived:** implied ACV €12,500; penetration 12–20%; **full market at current ACV = €7.5M to €12.5M**, at a 100% share that never occurs. Realistic ceiling on the current product: **€4–5M**.

You cannot sell your way out of a TAM ceiling. You can only price up, or move up the stack. The recommendation is both.

# Part III — Where the money actually is

## 3.1 The operator's real profit equation

> **Profit = (Rental revenue − Operating cost) + (Residual realised − Depreciation booked) − Holding cost**

Revenue management works the first bracket. **The second bracket is where the volatility, and frequently the entire annual margin, resides.**

Depreciation is *booked* as an estimate at acquisition and *realised* years later at remarketing. Book €300 per month and lose €380, and a full year of operating profit disappears. The reverse also holds — in 2021 and 2022 a great many rental operators made more money selling cars than renting them. The best-known instance of the downside is Hertz's write-down of roughly $2.9bn on its Tesla fleet: that is this bracket, at scale, with a professional fleet team in place.

Olivier's observation in section 5 of his review — that the true profit or loss comes from the resale of the vehicle, and rental income largely pays the running costs — is the correct reading of this industry. It is also under-stated in his memo relative to its importance.

## 3.2 The value at stake, sized

**Assumed, for illustration:** a 1,000-vehicle operator at €700 revenue per unit per month runs roughly €8.4M annual revenue. At the industry's characteristic **4% net margin** (as cited by Olivier), that is approximately **€336K of annual profit**.

**A 1% improvement in residual realisation**, on an average disposal value of €15,000 across a fleet turning over every 24 months, is worth roughly **€75K–150K per year** depending on churn rate.

That is a **20–45% improvement in net profit** from a single lever WeYield does not currently address. Against which WeYield today charges that same customer somewhere between €12,500 and €40,000 for rate optimisation.

**This gap is the entire strategic opportunity.** It is not an AI insight. AI is merely what makes it deliverable to a fragmented long tail.

## 3.3 Why the long tail has no tooling for this

The majors — Hertz, Avis, Enterprise, Europcar, Sixt — run in-house fleet and remarketing teams with treasury functions, OEM relationships and analysts. They solve this with people and spreadsheets.

The 600 to 1,000 operators in WeYield's market have none of that. Historically they did not need it, because **OEM buyback and repurchase programmes removed the residual decision entirely** — the manufacturer guaranteed the price.

**That protection contracted sharply after 2021.** Following the semiconductor shortage and the repricing of the used market, OEMs materially reduced guaranteed-buyback exposure. A large share of the long tail now carries residual risk it has never carried before, has no tooling for, and in many cases does not yet have on its management reporting.

**This is a structural gap that opened within the last five years and is currently unserved.**

# Part IV — Why now

Five conditions have only recently converged. Any three of them would make this interesting; all five make it urgent.

1. **The risk moved to the customer.** Buyback contraction since 2021 pushed residual exposure onto operators who had never held it.
2. **The risk got harder.** EV residual collapse and diesel demand shifts in Europe destroyed the intuitions that used to substitute for a model. Olivier's own point about European diesel demand and Middle Eastern supply chains dictating resale values is exactly this.
3. **The missing input became purchasable.** Used-vehicle market intelligence is now available by API — **Indicata** (Autorola) for live pan-European used stock, pricing and days-to-sell; **Autovista** for residual value forecasting; BCA and Manheim/Cox for auction data. Five years ago this was a data-licensing project. Today it is a partnership and an integration.
4. **The delivery cost collapsed.** Schema mapping, car-group normalisation, seasonality inference, onboarding, configuration and tier-one support — the costs that made a €12K account unservable — are precisely what language models are good at. This is the honest technical case for "AI-native", and it is a cost argument, not a capability argument.
5. **Standing still stopped being safe.** RateHighway — a partner — is building an expandable pricing-engine framework and has already plugged in a third-party engine. **If WeYield remains a pricing engine, it becomes a commodity plugin inside someone else's platform.** The only exit from that is upward, into a layer where RateHighway is not playing.

# Part V — The product

## 5.1 "Fleet P&L Copilot" — three surfaces and a governance layer

The name is deliberate. WeYield already markets a *Pricing Co-pilot* inside Pricing Insights; this extends the company's own vocabulary rather than importing a new one.

### A. The Fleet P&L — the artefact that changes who buys

Per car group, per station, per cohort: revenue, direct operating cost, **booked depreciation**, **marked-to-market residual**, and the delta between them.

No operator in this segment has this today. They have a rental report from the PMS and an accounting depreciation schedule from the finance system, and the two do not reconcile until the vehicle is physically sold — at which point the decision is two years old.

This single screen is what converts WeYield from a tool a clerk uses into a report an owner reads. WeYield's site already addresses the *CEO and Managing Director — strategy and financial performance* as a persona; it has, as yet, nothing a CEO reads. This is that artefact. **Build this first among the new surfaces**, because it changes the buyer without requiring the agent to be right about anything.

### B. The Decision Feed — one ranked daily list across all five levers

Each item carries a euro value, a confidence, and an explicit "what happens if you do nothing":

> **Price** — *Compact at NCE, pickup 12–19 Oct: hold. Pace is 8 points ahead of last year at 91% booked. Cutting now costs an estimated €4,100.*
>
> **Move** — *14 idle intermediates at LYS against a shortfall at NCE on the 18th. Transfer cost €47/unit, revenue upside €2,100.*
>
> **Sell** — *Your 2023 diesel Clios reach 24 months in November. French B-segment diesel residuals are off 3.1% over 90 days and days-to-sell is lengthening. De-fleet 40 units now rather than February: estimated €68K preserved.*

That third sentence is not producible by any product in this market today, and it is the one an owner forwards to their business partner.

### C. The Analyst — conversational, grounded, multilingual

Ask-your-data in plain language over the Performance Hub, every answer traceable to the underlying figures. Cheapest to build, strongest demo, cannot lose a customer money, and multilingual comes close to free — which matters materially across 50 countries. **This is the wedge, not the strategy.**

### D. The governance layer — the thing that makes it sellable

Guardrails set by the operator. Every recommendation logged with accept / modify / reject and the subsequent outcome. Full audit trail on any automated action.

**Position explicitly as explainable and governed, never autonomous.** The named adoption blocker in this industry is black-box distrust: operators are being asked to accept less visibility into how a rate was produced than a spreadsheet gave them. WeYield's entire heritage — the Academy, the coaches, teaching operators to think — makes *"the agent shows its reasoning and you keep the wheel"* the natural and differentiated position. **Make explainability the feature, not the apology.**

## 5.2 What must never be rebuilt

Olivier's review proposes rebuilding the offering natively in AI. That is right for part of the stack and wrong for the rest, and the distinction is worth holding firmly.

**Never rewrite:**

- **The integration lattice** — Wheels/Invensys, MyRentCar, RateHighway, PMS/CRS write-back. By the industry's own account, reliable two-way data exchange is the binding constraint in car rental automation, not modelling. Every integration already built is a mile of track laid.
- **The numeric forecasting core** — gradient-boosted models beat language models at numeric demand forecasting and will continue to. Confusing the interface layer with the numeric layer burns a year.
- **The rate-shop collection pipeline.**

**Rebuild AI-natively, as a new parallel stack:** the interface, the agent and recommendation layer, reporting, onboarding, configuration, support, and the coach's own workflow.

**The discipline that makes this work without a migration project:** from day one, every new feature ships in the new stack, and **the old stack receives no new features, ever — maintenance only**. Over eighteen months the new stack absorbs 60–70% of user-facing surface and the old one shrinks by attrition rather than by decision.

## 5.3 The data assets to assemble

| Asset | Status | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-tenant rate shop, pace, utilisation, fleet | **Held** — years of it, across 50+ countries | Instrument at daily grain per account; this is the moat |
| Forward demand signal | **Held** — ForwardKeys embedded; IATA under exploration | Olivier's own background here is an asset worth using |
| **Used-vehicle residual data** | **Missing** | **Partnership, not modelling.** Indicata is the strongest single candidate |
| Recommendation accept/reject telemetry | **Unknown — verify** | Cheap now, impossible to backfill |

**The combination of forward demand, realised pace and used-vehicle residuals does not exist anywhere for this segment.** That is the defensible statement of the moat, and it is worth more than any model.

# Part VI — The sequence

The order below is chosen so each stage funds the next. Emmanuel has flat EBITDA, no cash and no raise in progress; any plan requiring new capital will be received politely and shelved.

## Horizon 1 — months 0 to 6: repair the economics

**Objective: EBITDA from ~0% to ~25% without touching the product roadmap.**

1. **Validate the thesis before building anything.** Ten customer calls in one week, testing the three kill-risks in Part VII. This gates everything below.
2. **Reprice the top 20 accounts onto a per-vehicle model.** ~95% incremental margin, zero new code, lands within one renewal cycle. Run $4 / $6 / $8 per vehicle per month against actual fleet sizes.
3. **AI inside the company** — support, onboarding, configuration, QA, first-line coaching prep. Fast, cheap, near-certain, and it requires no product decision.
4. **Ship the Analyst.** Two-week build with a two-person AI-native pod. Its second purpose is to prove the delivery-cost thesis to the board.
5. **Instrument the loop.** Log every recommendation, the user's response, and the outcome. Without this there is no agent that learns and no outcome price that can be defended.
6. **Write the coaching methodology down as specification** — how a coach reads a pace curve, what triggers hold-versus-cut, the diagnostic sequence when a station underperforms. Worth doing even if AI disappeared tomorrow; it is how coaching survives a coach leaving. It is also the training corpus.

## Horizon 2 — months 6 to 18: change the object and the buyer

**Objective: revenue €1.95M → €3.2M, ACV €16K → €22K, buyer moves to the owner.**

7. **Close the residual-data partnership.** Indicata or equivalent. This is a commercial negotiation, and it is on the critical path for everything below.
8. **Ship the Fleet P&L.** The owner's report. Changes the buyer before the agent has to be right about anything.
9. **Ship the Decision Feed** across all five levers, guardrailed and logged.
10. **Move the pricing model** to base plus per-vehicle, with an outcome-linked component where attribution can be defended. The Aircar +6% RPD result is the raw material; what is missing is account-level attribution.
11. **Hire salespeople three and four** — but only now. At €12,500 ACV a rep returning ~€85K of new ARR barely covers their own cost in year one. At €22K ACV the payback is roughly twelve months and the hire works.

## Horizon 3 — months 18 to 36: compound the data

**Objective: revenue €4.8M at 30%+ EBITDA, and a genuine strategic option.**

12. **Cross-tenant benchmarking as a product.** "Comparable operators in comparable markets are holding rates 4% above you into October half-term." This is the asset no foundation model can acquire and no funded newcomer can buy.
13. **Answer-layer monitoring.** Extend Market Radar to shop the AI assistants: when a traveller asks for the cheapest midsize at your customer's airport, does the customer appear, at what rate, in what class, with what fees, and is it still bookable? Nobody sells this, it needs no new integrations, and it is a rate-shopping problem — which is what WeYield already is.
14. **Re-scope the coach** from analyst to supervisor of agents across five times the accounts. This is the gross-margin unlock in the whole plan, and it depends entirely on step 6 having been done properly.

# Part VII — The three things that would kill this thesis

A plan without these is a pitch. Each is testable within two weeks and **all three should be tested before any code is written.**

## Kill-risk 1 — buyback exposure

**If most WeYield customers still operate under OEM buyback or guaranteed-repurchase programmes, residual risk is not theirs and the Fleet P&L has no buyer.**

*Test:* ten customer calls. What proportion of your fleet is on buyback, on operating lease, and owned at risk? How has that mix moved since 2021?

*If it fails:* the fleet thesis narrows to the owned-at-risk segment only. Size that segment before proceeding. The move-and-substitute levers remain valid regardless.

## Kill-risk 2 — the fleet *financials* may not exist where WeYield can reach them

Fleet counts and utilisation already flow into Performance Hub and Revenue Horizon, per WeYield's own product pages. The open question is the financial fields. **If acquisition cost, in-service date, mileage, specification and disposal are not present in the PMS systems WeYield already integrates with, there is no data foundation** and the product degrades into a data-entry exercise, which will not be adopted.

*Test:* audit the actual field coverage across the five most common PMS integrations in the base.

*If it fails:* the first release is a fleet-data ingestion product, and the timeline extends by six to nine months.

## Kill-risk 3 — fleet size distribution

**If the median customer fleet is under roughly 200 vehicles, de-fleet decisions are too lumpy to optimise** and the value per account will not support the ACV step-up.

*Test:* fleet size by account across the base — which is the same data required for the repricing exercise in Horizon 1, so this costs nothing extra.

*If it fails:* the fleet product is a top-quartile offer, not a platform offer. That is still a viable business; it is a different one, and it changes the sales model.

# Part VIII — The financial shape

**All figures below are illustrative and rest on assumed cost splits that must be verified against WeYield's accounts before any of this is presented to a third party.**

## 8.1 The starting position

**Given** (Olivier, 10 September 2026): €1.5M revenue · ~120 customers · 9 employees, 2 in sales · 600–1,000 addressable · 10–15% annual growth · EBITDA flat to contracting · standing acquisition offer ~€2M · exit intent in 2–3 years.

**Derived:** implied ACV **€12,500** · penetration **12–20%** · the offer values the business at **1.3× revenue** · Rule of 40 score approximately **15**.

## 8.2 A note on the ACV discrepancy — and its likely resolution

Olivier reports an average deal size "under €40,000". €1.5M across 120 customers implies €12,500. Both cannot be the average.

**The probable reconciliation** — to be confirmed, not assumed — is a standard SaaS distribution: roughly 20 accounts at €35–40K accounting for about half of revenue, and roughly 100 accounts at €7–8K accounting for the other half. If that shape is correct, then **the €40K figure describes the top tier, the €12.5K figure is the mean, and both are true.**

This matters operationally: it means the repricing opportunity is concentrated in 20 accounts, and it means roughly 100 accounts must become servable at near-zero marginal cost or be deliberately shed.

## 8.3 Three-year shape

| | Today | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Customers | 120 | 125 | 150 | 190 |
| Blended ACV | €12.5K | €16K | €22K | €26K |
| Revenue | €1.5M | €1.95M | €3.2M | €4.8M |
| EBITDA margin | ~0% | ~26% | ~28% | ~31% |
| Growth rate | 10–15% | 30% | 64% | 50% |
| Rule of 40 | ~15 | ~56 | ~92 | ~81 |
| Headcount | 9 | 10 | 13 | 16 |
| **Indicative valuation** | **€2M (1.3×)** | — | — | **€19–24M (4–5×)** |

**The two structural points this table makes:**

1. **Revenue growth is driven by ACV, not by logo count.** Customers grow 58% over three years; revenue grows 220%. That is the whole argument for changing the object and the buyer rather than hiring salespeople.
2. **Headcount grows 78% while revenue grows 220%.** That is the decoupling of cost from sales that Olivier's review identifies as the objective — and it is achieved by *not hiring*, which matters given the French employment context.

## 8.4 On the €2M offer

**€2M on €1.5M of revenue is 1.3×. That is not a lowball — it is the correct price for a business growing 15% at zero margin.** The offer is not a valuation of WeYield; it is a measurement of the current operating model. Every recovered EBITDA point moves it, and nothing in the AI narrative moves it on its own.

**The useful observation for the decision:** both credible end-states — remaining the independent AI-native RM layer for the long tail, or becoming an attractive acquisition for a consolidator or a rental operating-system vendor — **run through the identical eighteen months of work.** Data instrumented, methodology codified, fleet layer shipped, integrations deepened. There is no strategic fork requiring a decision today.

The real risk is not choosing the wrong AI strategy. It is spending 2027 deciding.

# Part IX — Organisation

## 9.1 The constraint that shapes everything

Emmanuel runs a French company with a French team. **Five engineers cannot simply be cut** — *licenciement économique* is slow, expensive, requires documented justification, and signals distress to customers and acquirers alike.

Therefore **the cost decoupling comes from not hiring, never from cutting.** The nine stay; the next nine are never hired; the work doubles.

## 9.2 The re-scope

The framing that makes this acceptable to the team, and which happens also to be true:

> The engineers are not the problem. They are on the wrong side of the line. Move them onto the moat.

| Function | Today | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Product / engineering | ~5, building features with unproven ROI | 3–4 on integrations, data pipeline and the numeric core — the part that is never rewritten and is the highest-skill work in the company |
| AI-native pod | 0 | 2–3 forward-deployed engineers owning interface, agents and onboarding |
| Sales | 2 | 4–5, hired only after ACV supports the payback |
| Coaching | Analysts | Fleet advisors supervising agents across 5× the accounts |
| Data partnerships | 0 | 1 — owns Indicata, IATA, and the benchmark product |

## 9.3 The cultural point

Olivier's review notes correctly that this requires a reinvention of the company's DNA. The specific reinvention is narrower than it sounds and can be stated in one line:

**WeYield stops being a company that ships features and becomes a company that ships decisions.** The unit of work changes from "we released X" to "we recommended Y, the customer accepted it, and it was worth €Z." Everything in the instrumentation plan exists to make that measurable.

# Part X — Six decisions for Monday

1. **Commission the ten-call validation** on buyback exposure, PMS fleet-data coverage and fleet-size distribution. One week. Everything else is gated on this.
2. **Pull the fleet size behind every one of the top 20 accounts** and run the per-vehicle pricing model against them. If those numbers cannot be produced, that is itself the most important finding of the week.
3. **Freeze the old stack.** No new features, effective immediately. Maintenance only.
4. **Stand up the two-person AI-native pod** and give it the Analyst as its first delivery, on a two-week clock.
5. **Turn on recommendation telemetry** — accept, modify, reject, and the outcome. Cheap today, impossible to reconstruct later.
6. **Open the conversation with Indicata.** It sits on the critical path for Horizon 2 and commercial negotiations take longer than integrations.
